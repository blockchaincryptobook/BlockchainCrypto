## Bitcoin: What is it? <a href="#bitcoin-what-is-it" id="bitcoin-what-is-it"></a>

Bitcoin is both a platform and a digital currency. When referring to it as a platform—which includes the network and the protocol that enables the digital currency—we use Bitcoin with an uppercase B, and when referring to it as a unit of currency, we use bitcoin with a lowercase b. In today’s class, we will largely focus on the digital currency, that is, bitcoin, and eventually we will turn to Bitcoin as a platform.

### Bitcoin as a digital currency <a href="#bitcoin-as-a-digital-currency" id="bitcoin-as-a-digital-currency"></a>

Bitcoins are created using particular software that operates on a network of computers worldwide. This open-source software is available for anyone to download [1](https://bitcoin.org/en/download) and install, and once they do so and begin using it, they become a node on the Bitcoin network that helps keep the digital currency functioning. In principle, even a single computer running the Bitcoin software is sufficient to create and keep a record of the currency, and this was indeed the case when Bitcoin first began. However, when there is no one else to transact with, the currency has no value, and the record-keeping cannot be trusted. For the currency to be valuable and the system to be reliable, multiple nodes must exist on the network. Today, the network typically has more than 23,000 nodes at any given time, a number that fluctuates with the value of bitcoin. A year ago, there were around 19,000 nodes, and two years ago, about 16,000.

Because Bitcoin relies on cryptographic techniques to enable its use and maintain its ledger, it is often referred to as a cryptocurrency. The unit of currency is bitcoins or BTC, where each BTC is equal to 100 million satoshis. Importantly, there is no entity that authorizes the currency’s creation; it is not issued by any centralized authority. Approximately every ten minutes, 3.125 BTC are created, and about four years from April 2024, this number will drop to 1.5625 BTC every ten minutes. Four years after that, the number will halve again, and this pattern will continue. The entire creation process began with the genesis block, which generated 50 BTC. For reasons unknown, the next block with 50 BTC was created six days after the genesis block. After that point, blocks and the bitcoins contained in them have been generated with an average interval of ten minutes.

During the first few days after Bitcoin’s launch on January 3, 2009, Satoshi Nakamoto was the only person running the software. Another participant joined the network on January 12, 2009. With so few people involved in the early days, those operating the initial nodes accumulated large amounts of bitcoin. Satoshi Nakamoto alone accumulated about one million BTC before disappearing in April 2011. Over time, it is said that many early holders did not care much about the bitcoins they had earned and subsequently lost them. It is estimated that about three to four million BTC have been lost forever, a figure that includes those who intentionally kept their coins but later lost access due to forgotten or misplaced passwords.

In a future class, we will examine who gets to keep the bitcoins that are created or “mined” every ten minutes. As of now, about 19.91 million BTC have been mined [2](https://www.blockchain.com/charts/total-bitcoins), up from 19.75 million a year earlier. In total, approximately 21 million BTC will ever be created, and once that limit is reached—expected around the year 2140—no additional bitcoins will be produced. When sending bitcoin to someone, it is typical to include a transaction fee. While not strictly mandatory, transactions without a fee may fail to be processed. We will explore this in more detail in a later class.

## How have bitcoins come to be seen as money? <a href="#how-have-bitcoins-come-to-be-seen-as-money" id="how-have-bitcoins-come-to-be-seen-as-money"></a>

To understand how bitcoins have come to be regarded as money, we must first clarify what money itself is and then examine the ways in which bitcoin aligns with that understanding. There are at least three different approaches to answering the question “What is money?” One approach is to trace the various forms money has taken throughout human history. A second approach is to consider the features of money as described by economists. A third approach is to explore how sociologists have attempted to understand money. Once we examine these perspectives, we can evaluate whether and how bitcoins qualify as money.

#### Forms of money <a href="#forms-of-money" id="forms-of-money"></a>

Money has taken various forms throughout human history, evolving to meet the needs of societies as they grew more complex.

**Commodity money**

Commodity money refers to a form of payment in which the item used has intrinsic value and is widely valued by many. In such cases, the terms of payment essentially amount to offering something that is inherently useful and desirable. Examples include cattle, grain, precious metals such as gold and silver, and even Rai stones—large stone discs, some human-sized or larger, used on the Micronesian island of Yap.[3](https://www.bbc.com/travel/article/20180502-the-tiny-island-with-human-sized-money)&#x20;

<figure><img src=".gitbook/assets/Rai_stone.jpg" alt="" width="188"><figcaption></figcaption></figure>

The intrinsic value of these commodities arises from their usefulness outside their role as a medium of exchange. Cattle provide milk, meat, and labor for agricultural work. Grains are edible and essential for sustenance. Precious metals serve as raw materials for jewelry and various manufacturing processes. Rai stones, although debated as to whether they represent commodity money or fiat money, have long been recognized as possessing symbolic value apart from their use in transactions. Rounder Rai stones, for example, hold religious significance and are used in ceremonial contexts. Their value also historically increased when their extraction and transportation from Palau to Yap involved difficulty or carried an interesting narrative. When a European explorer later discovered a more efficient extraction method, the perceived value of these stones declined, illustrating how their worth was partially tied to the cultural meaning surrounding their origin.

**Representative money**

Representative money involves payment through a token that itself has no intrinsic value but can be exchanged for something that does. In effect, the terms of payment imply, “I will pay you with something that has a claim on an item of intrinsic worth.” Prior to 1971, for instance, the US dollar bill could be exchanged at the US Treasury for an equivalent amount of gold. During that period, the dollar operated as representative money because it served as a claim on a valuable underlying asset.

**Fiat money**

Fiat money, by contrast, is created through government decree and is not backed by anything intrinsically valuable. The terms of payment in this case effectively state, “I will pay you with something that has no intrinsic value but has been declared legal tender by the government.” Such money maintains its usefulness because governments require taxes to be paid in it, compelling its acceptance across the economy. The US dollar is fiat money, as are the currencies of Canada, China, Great Britain, Russia, India, and many others.

Some people use a broader, though incorrect, definition of representative money by treating any token that has a claim on something valuable or on fiat currency as representative money. From this perspective, a money order might be considered representative money. However, this is inaccurate because a money order does not represent a claim on something intrinsically valuable, and therefore does not meet the definition of genuine representative money.

**Barter**

Barter involves directly exchanging goods or services of value without using money. Its terms of payment can be summarized as, “Let us directly exchange different things of value.” A farmer, for example, might trade a sack of rice to a carpenter in return for repairing a broken door; neither the rice nor the carpentry service constitutes money, yet both clearly hold value. Similarly, in a modern context, a web designer might create a website for a dentist in exchange for dental care. Again, no monetary instrument is involved, but each party receives something useful.

Although barter is not truly a form of money, discussions of money often include it because it highlights the limitations that money overcomes. Barter requires a “double coincidence of wants,” meaning both parties must simultaneously want what the other is offering. Money removes this constraint by providing a widely accepted medium of exchange, allowing transactions to proceed even when desires do not align directly.

**Is bitcoin any of the different forms of money?**

Let us now examine whether bitcoin fits into any of the traditional forms of money. Bitcoin does not have any use outside its role as money or a financial instrument, which means it cannot be considered commodity money. It is also not a genuine form of representative money, as it was not created as a token redeemable for something intrinsically valuable, nor was it designed to function as a token with a claim on fiat currency under the broader interpretation of representative money.

In general, bitcoin is not fiat money, because no government decreed it as an official currency when it was created. However, there was a brief period when two countries did recognize bitcoin as legal tender. El Salvador adopted bitcoin as legal tender on September 7, 2021, alongside the U.S. dollar, and the Central African Republic followed in April 2022. During that time, bitcoin qualified as fiat money in those nations because its acceptance was mandated by government decree. This is no longer the case. The Central African Republic repealed its bitcoin legal tender law in March 2023 as part of its alignment with a regional monetary union, and El Salvador, under pressure from the IMF, dropped bitcoin as official currency in February 2025. As a result, no country today recognizes bitcoin as fiat money.

To understand how bitcoin functioned as fiat currency, it is helpful to examine the case of El Salvador. The motivations behind El Salvador’s decision to adopt bitcoin were multifaceted. One motivation was the belief that it would foster more accessible and affordable financial services. Remittances sent by Salvadorans working abroad make up roughly 25% of the national GDP, and physical transfers incur high fees averaging around 10.2%.[6](https://nupoliticalreview.org/2024/02/28/the-truth-behind-bitcoin-country/) Although online bank transfers are cheaper, only about 11% of the population had mobile accounts. The expectation was that bitcoin would reduce remittance costs, but this outcome never materialized. By 2022, only 1.7% of remittances were sent using cryptocurrencies,[7](https://www.merit.unu.edu/el-salvador-bitcoin-policy/) despite promises of significantly lower transaction fees.

Another motivation was the belief that bitcoin adoption would attract foreign investors to develop geothermal energy from El Salvador’s volcanoes to power cryptocurrency mining. With 170 volcanoes, the country appeared to have significant geothermal potential. In May 2024, Reuters reported that El Salvador had mined 471 bitcoins since 2021 by diverting 1.5 megawatts of electricity from a 102-megawatt state-owned geothermal plant.[8](https://cointelegraph.com/news/el-salvador-mines-bitcoin-volcanic-energy) Yet, the country was unable to draw foreign investment into geothermal-powered mining. Only about 27% of El Salvador’s energy generation currently comes from geothermal sources, and expanding infrastructure would be expensive and require water—a resource in short supply. Fossil fuels remain a cheaper option, making the geothermal vision difficult to realize.[9](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/culture-features/el-salvador-bitcoin-law-volcano-bonds-nayib-bukele-1388472/)

Although the geothermal investment push faltered, the idea was not abandoned entirely. A public–private partnership was announced to build a power generation park that would harness solar, wind, and geothermal energy to provide 241 megawatts for bitcoin mining. As part of this initiative, a mining pool called Lava Pool launched in October 2023.[10](https://bitcoinmagazine.com/business/volcano-energy-bitcoin-mining-pool-goes-live)

A further motivation for bitcoin adoption was the desire to brand El Salvador as an innovative nation. The public–private partnership mentioned above was expected to raise $1 billion through a bond issue, intended as a test of whether global investors viewed El Salvador as forward-thinking. However, only $250 million was raised—and not through bonds—leaving unclear how the country was perceived in terms of innovation. Nonetheless, there were indications that other countries saw El Salvador as a potential model. Argentina sought guidance from El Salvador on bitcoin adoption,[11](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/cryptocurrency/24/05/39036114/argentina-seeks-guidance-from-el-salvador-on-bitcoin-adoption) and Panama expressed similar interest.

There were also some positive outcomes from El Salvador’s bitcoin initiative, even if they were not directly tied to the initial motivations. To support the transition, El Salvador accumulated bitcoin reserves by purchasing one bitcoin daily since March 16, 2024. By August 2025, these holdings had reached an estimated value of $770 million,[12](https://news.bit2me.com/en/770-million-in-BTC-reserves-in-El-Salvador-after-rally) creating the possibility of long-term financial benefits if the value of bitcoin continues to rise. Tourism also received a boost, as bitcoin legalization increased international visibility and attracted cryptocurrency enthusiasts. Between 2019 and 2023, El Salvador became the fourth fastest-growing tourism destination, with a 35% increase in visitors. In 2024 alone, tourism was projected to grow by another 22% year-over-year.[13](https://www.onesafe.io/blog/el-salvador-bitcoin-tourism-crime-impact)

Despite these positives, the general sentiment is that El Salvador did not fare well in its experimentation with bitcoin as a national currency.[14](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/el-salvador-adopted-bitcoin-as-an-official-currency-salvadorans-mostly-shrugged) Several indicators point to its lack of success. One year into the initiative, only 2% of the population was actively using the Chivo wallet, even though it had been downloaded by about 60% of the population, aided by a $30 incentive per download. Only 14% of the country’s businesses had conducted bitcoin transactions, and just 3% reported seeing any business value in using bitcoin.

Supporters of the initiative argued that time was needed for adoption to grow,[16](https://cointelegraph.com/news/dips-be-damned-el-salvador-is-stronger-because-of-bitcoin) and the government undertook efforts to educate the population. El Salvador launched bitcoin training programs for 80,000 civil servants,[18](https://cryptoslate.com/el-salvador-launches-bitcoin-training-for-80000-public-servants/) aiming to equip public sector employees with the knowledge needed to handle bitcoin-based services and promote wider use of the currency. However, these efforts were cut short when El Salvador amended its bitcoin legal tender law as part of an agreement with the IMF for a $1.4 billion loan. Whether the adoption measures would have eventually succeeded remains unknown.

One explanation for the initiative’s shortcomings lies in the contrast between El Salvador’s centralized approach and bitcoin’s underlying decentralized philosophy. This mismatch created practical and ideological tensions. For instance, the Chivo wallet functions as a custodial wallet, meaning that the government holds custody of users’ bitcoins. This setup allows the government to monitor transactions and even censor activity. Reports indicate that the government froze the wallets of individuals who received transactions from Wasabi, a bitcoin wallet designed to enhance privacy by mixing unspent coins with others to obscure transaction histories.[19](https://news.bitcoin.com/reports-claim-chivo-bitcoin-wallet-is-flagging-wasabi-transactions-in-el-salvador/)

In sum, bitcoin does not fit neatly into any of the traditional monetary categories. It is neither commodity money, nor representative money, nor fiat money.

#### Features of money as per economists <a href="#features-of-money-as-per-economists" id="features-of-money-as-per-economists"></a>

Economists generally define money as possessing three key features: it serves as a medium of exchange, a store of value, and a unit of account. As a medium of exchange, money allows individuals to obtain goods or services in return for payment, and others are willing to accept it in exchange for what they offer. As a store of value, money retains purchasing power over time, enabling people to save and remain confident that its value will not rapidly erode. As a unit of account, money provides a consistent measure for valuing goods, services, assets, and debts. To fulfill this role, money must be divisible, countable, and fungible—that is, one unit must be exchangeable for another identical unit without loss.

Bitcoin meets some of these characteristics to varying degrees. It functions reasonably well as a medium of exchange and, in certain cases, can serve as a store of value. However, its suitability as a unit of account remains limited.

**Can bitcoin serve as a medium of exchange?**

Bitcoin functions as a reasonably good medium of exchange, as illustrated in the linked video [49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n9MXX6dXKPM). Several factors contribute to its appeal in this role. One major factor is the absence of an intermediary or third party in transactions. This has two important consequences. First, because no central authority controls the fees, transaction costs are determined by the supply and demand for processing space on the network. In principle, this market-driven mechanism should help keep fees competitive. In practice, while the fees may be low relative to the value transacted, their dollar amounts have sometimes been high. For example, in April 2024, average transaction fees exceeded $128, though at present they are mostly under $1 (see chart [20](https://ycharts.com/indicators/bitcoin_average_transaction_fee)). Fees can also be reduced by using the Lightning Network, an additional layer built on top of Bitcoin that aggregates smaller transactions between two parties and records only the final settlement on the blockchain. Video [50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rrr_zPmEiME) provides an introductory overview of how this system works.

The second consequence of eliminating intermediaries is a significantly lower potential for censorship. When a third party controls transactions, it can approve, reject, or restrict them. As the referenced video notes, for example, transfers of money out of China are tightly regulated and require official approval. A traditional intermediary can also impose restrictions on the types of transactions allowed, the individuals one may transact with, or the amount one is permitted to move or hold. Such censorship risks do not exist on the Bitcoin network, which increases its attractiveness as a medium of exchange.

Bitcoin’s pseudonymous nature also appeals to individuals who value privacy. While anonymity can attract malicious actors, it can also serve law-abiding individuals who simply prefer not to disclose how they use their money.

Another important factor supporting bitcoin as a medium of exchange is the growth in its global user base. The more people willing to accept and transact in a particular currency, the more effective that currency becomes in facilitating exchange. Estimating the number of bitcoin holders is difficult, however, because there is no universal identifier linking specific people to specific wallets. **Can bitcoin serve as a store of value?**

Bitcoin has increasingly been viewed as a potential store of value for several reasons, including its use as a hedge against inflation, its similarities with gold, and the expanding role of institutional investment through spot Bitcoin ETFs. As a hedge against inflation, bitcoin has proven appealing in certain contexts. For example, many Argentinians have come to see bitcoin as a better store of value than their national currency, the peso, which has experienced significant volatility that often exceeds fluctuations in the country’s economic conditions. As highlighted in video [51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=s7tzuE9kQeQ), bitcoin’s relative stability compared to the peso has made it attractive for preserving value. In the province of Mendoza, residents are even able to pay taxes in bitcoin,[24](https://en.cryptonomist.ch/2022/08/30/argentina-mendoza-now-accepts-bitcoin-to-pay-taxes/) which further reflects bitcoin’s growing presence not only as a store of value but also as a medium of exchange within Argentina. These developments show that bitcoin is emerging as a store of value for those who face unstable local currencies, using it as protection against severe currency fluctuations.

Bitcoin is also frequently compared to gold, based on several shared characteristics that contribute to gold’s role as a long-standing store of value. Gold is valuable partly because it has a limited supply; the easily accessible gold has already been mined, and the rate of new production has plateaued. Extracting the remaining gold is increasingly difficult. Gold’s demand is steady or rising, driven by its use in jewelry, electronics, medical equipment, and other industries. Furthermore, gold’s value is not controlled by any government. It cannot be arbitrarily devalued or increased in supply by policy decisions. In addition, gold is portable and retains its value across borders, making it universally recognized and accepted.

Bitcoin shares many of these characteristics. It is scarce, with only 3.125 BTC created approximately every ten minutes and a fixed supply cap of 21 million bitcoins. Most bitcoin has already been mined, much like gold. Bitcoin’s usefulness for transactions continues to expand, supported by innovations such as the Lightning Network and integrations into major payment platforms like PayPal, Visa, and Mastercard. Bitcoin also operates outside government control, making it resistant to the risks of political manipulation. Like gold, it is portable, and its value can be transferred globally with ease. Proponents who view bitcoin as “digital gold” argue that volatility does not disqualify it as a store of value. Instead, they interpret volatility as a characteristic of an early-stage asset that may stabilize as adoption widens. Over time, they argue, bitcoin could function much like gold: scarce, portable, globally recognized, and capable of retaining value.

Another significant force behind bitcoin’s growing reputation as a store of value is the approval of spot Bitcoin ETFs in major markets such as the United States. Prior to this development, investors seeking Bitcoin exposure were limited to futures-based ETFs, which did not hold bitcoin directly but instead owned contracts tied to its future price. These futures-based products introduced inefficiencies because contracts must be regularly rolled over, generating additional costs and causing performance to diverge from bitcoin’s actual market price. Spot Bitcoin ETFs, by contrast, hold bitcoin directly, allowing investors to track its price more accurately and enabling both institutional and retail investors to gain regulated exposure without managing wallets or custody. By lowering barriers to entry and broadening access, spot ETFs reinforce the perception of bitcoin as a legitimate long-term investment asset and store of value.

It is important to clarify that the approval of spot Bitcoin ETFs did not make bitcoin itself a security. Bitcoin is classified as a commodity, similar to gold or oil, and its price is determined by supply and demand rather than any promise of profit from an issuing entity. A security, according to the Howey Test in U.S. law, is defined as an investment contract in which individuals invest money with the expectation of profit derived primarily from the efforts of others. ETF shares meet this definition because when an investor buys shares in a Bitcoin ETF—or a gold ETF, for that matter—they are not buying the underlying commodity directly. Instead, they are purchasing a security issued by the ETF provider, whose management efforts affect the value of the shares. Thus, while the ETF is a security, the underlying asset—bitcoin—remains a commodity, mirroring the same relationship that exists between gold and gold ETFs.

Taken together, these developments suggest that bitcoin is increasingly being positioned as a store of value. Its role as a hedge in inflation-prone economies, its conceptual parallels to gold, and its growing institutional acceptance through financial instruments such as spot ETFs all contribute to its rising legitimacy. More individuals and institutions are beginning to view bitcoin as “digital gold.” At the same time, bitcoin has not yet achieved universal acceptance as a dependable store of value. Its price volatility far exceeds that of most traditional assets, making it difficult to rely on for consistently preserving purchasing power. Adoption as a safe-haven asset remains uneven, centered primarily in countries facing currency crises rather than globally. Regulatory uncertainty also affects confidence, as governments continue to debate how bitcoin should be classified and governed. Moreover, bitcoin depends heavily on digital infrastructure, which makes it more fragile than physical stores of value such as gold. These limitations indicate that while bitcoin is increasingly perceived as a store of value, it has not yet fully matured into one.

**Can bitcoin serve as a unit of account?**

Although bitcoin is technically capable of serving as a unit of account, it does not function as one in practice. Financial accounting in every country is conducted using the national currency, such as the U.S. dollar in the United States. Even when bitcoin was recognized as legal tender in El Salvador, the population continued to use the U.S. dollar as its unit of account. Article 6 of El Salvador’s Bitcoin Law explicitly stated, “For accounting purposes, the USD will be used as the reference currency.”[25](https://www.cato.org/blog/bitcoin-law-counterfeit-free-choice-currency) One major reason bitcoin is not widely used as a unit of account is its high volatility. If a business were to keep its books in bitcoin, the value of its revenues, expenses, and profits would fluctuate constantly with bitcoin’s price, making financial reporting unstable and unreliable.

**Does bitcoin possess the features of money?**

In summary, when bitcoin is evaluated against the features of money, it appears to be an emerging form of currency. It functions reasonably well as a medium of exchange and, in certain circumstances, can act as a store of value. However, there is currently no evidence that bitcoin is used as a unit of account. Thus, while bitcoin fulfills some aspects of what economists consider money, it does not yet satisfy all criteria.

#### Money as per sociologists <a href="#money-as-per-sociologists" id="money-as-per-sociologists"></a>

For sociologists, something qualifies as money when certain conditions are met.[26](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/02632764211049826) First, there must be a shared belief within a community that others will accept it for trade. Second, it does not need to possess any usefulness beyond facilitating transactions; its value derives from the level of desire or demand for it. Third, it must be used by a sufficiently wide economic community, with its effectiveness as money increasing as that community expands. The U.S. dollar, for instance, is accepted almost everywhere and serves as a prominent example of this principle. From this sociological viewpoint, money is understood not as a commodity with intrinsic value or merely something decreed by the state, but as a social institution built on belief and trust.

**Can bitcoin be seen as money as per the view of sociologists?**

Yes, bitcoin can be viewed as money according to sociologists. A community believing in and using bitcoin for trade has existed since a few years after bitcoin’s introduction. The online Silk Road marketplace accelerated the growth of this community, and even after its shutdown, many people outside that context continued to embrace bitcoin as money. This community has only grown since then. Although bitcoin has no usefulness beyond its role as money, this poses no problem under the sociological perspective. Moreover, like the U.S. dollar, the community of bitcoin users spans geographic borders. It is believed, for example, that Russia has used bitcoin to skirt international sanctions, and the country even approved a law allowing crypto to be used for global payments during sanctions pressure.[27](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/07/30/russia-considers-legalizing-crypto-as-a-form-of-payment-amid-sanctions.html) Sociologists emphasize that bitcoin operates as a social institution rooted in belief and trust. They argue that demand for bitcoin is likely to grow because it emerged as an alternative to state-controlled money at a time when trust in governments continues to erode. As global trade becomes more borderless, the appeal of a currency that can be accepted seamlessly across the world strengthens, and bitcoin fits this role well.

## Motivation for Bitcoin <a href="#motivation-for-bitcoin" id="motivation-for-bitcoin"></a>

If traditional money already existed, why was another form of money needed? The motivations for bitcoin’s creation, which we have discussed since our first meeting, offer some answers and also shed light on why Satoshi Nakamoto disappeared shortly after bitcoin gained adoption. At least two reasons are frequently cited as motivations for creating Bitcoin.

### Elimination of intermediaries <a href="#elimination-of-intermediaries" id="elimination-of-intermediaries"></a>

Many business and personal transactions rely on intermediaries. Most people do not frequently handle cash; instead, they use checks, credit cards, and electronic payment systems, all of which involve trusted third parties such as banks, major credit card companies like Visa, MasterCard, and American Express, or platforms such as PayPal. Two main issues arise from the involvement of intermediaries: fees and censorship.

#### Fees <a href="#fees" id="fees"></a>

Third parties possess considerable market power, enabling them to charge significant fees that customers ultimately pay, either directly or indirectly. Visa and Mastercard, for example, were among the most profitable companies in the world in 2023, with net margins of 57 percent and 43 percent, respectively. In theory, Bitcoin was designed to reduce these fees by eliminating powerful intermediaries. While it avoids a few dominant third parties, the Bitcoin ecosystem nonetheless includes other actors—such as node operators—whose participation influences the supply and demand dynamics that determine transaction fees. This dynamic sometimes leads to high fees, making bitcoin less efficient for everyday payments compared to credit cards. For example, paying a transaction fee of around $3.65 on May 16, 2025 for a Starbucks coffee compares unfavorably to a 2.5 percent credit card fee. Still, saving on fees was a relatively minor motivation compared to others.

#### Censorship <a href="#censorship" id="censorship"></a>

As noted earlier, relying on intermediaries introduces censorship risk. When WikiLeaks released classified information about the U.S. government, authorities worked with Visa, MasterCard, and PayPal to block donations to WikiLeaks via credit card or PayPal. Donations fell by 90 percent almost immediately. Because Bitcoin does not depend on such intermediaries, it eliminates this censorship risk.

### Mistrust in fiat currencies <a href="#mistrust-in-fiat-currencies" id="mistrust-in-fiat-currencies"></a>

Governments and rulers have historically devalued their fiat currencies. Rulers using commodity money, such as gold- or silver-based coins, often reduced the amount of precious metal in coins while trying to preserve their nominal value. This encouraged people to hoard older coins and triggered black markets. Modern governments expand money supplies to deal with economic crises, such as the U.S. actions during the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic. These actions often stem from efforts to save large financial institutions whose risky behavior contributed to the crisis in the first place. Although these institutions were made whole, ordinary citizens suffered the consequences. When governments increase the supply of fiat money, its value decreases and purchasing power declines. Governments also occasionally demonetize currencies, as India did in 2016 when it abruptly invalidated 500- and 1000-rupee notes. This caused significant economic hardship and even deaths, as people waited in long lines to exchange bills or were denied medical care for attempting to use demonetized currency.[28](https://cointelegraph.com/news/bitcoin-emerges-winner-as-indian-demonetisation-declared-a-total-failure-by-rbi) Bitcoin was created to shield individuals from such harmful government actions. It enables people to take control of their financial destinies without being subject to government policies seen as arbitrary or unfair. This protective function was a major motivation behind its creation. Videos documenting how Venezuelans used bitcoin to cope with economic collapse further illustrate this point.[30](https://www.reuters.com/technology/venezuelas-economy-regresses-crypto-fills-gaps-2021-06-22/)

## Unknown identity of Satoshi Nakamoto and disappearance <a href="#unknown-identity-of-satoshi-nakamoto-and-disappearance" id="unknown-identity-of-satoshi-nakamoto-and-disappearance"></a>

Satoshi Nakamoto’s identity remains unknown. The individual—or group—behind the name communicated fluently in English, as evidenced by early forum discussions. Numerous attempts and speculations have been made to identify Satoshi. In 2014, Newsweek published a cover story[31](https://www.newsweek.com/2014/03/14/face-behind-bitcoin-247957.html) naming a man they claimed was Satoshi Nakamoto, which caused widespread attention. However, that individual publicly denied being the creator of Bitcoin.

Some people have even claimed to be Satoshi themselves, but the true identity remains a mystery. Instead of coming forward to receive recognition for the invention, Satoshi chose to remain anonymous and eventually disappeared. A linked video explores possible motivations, though additional reasons may exist. The following list is more complete, though not necessarily exhaustive.

### Avoiding conviction for criminal activity <a href="#avoiding-conviction-for-criminal-activity" id="avoiding-conviction-for-criminal-activity"></a>

Creating a new currency can prompt governments to consider it a threat to their official monetary systems. Some nations outlaw private currencies altogether. Individuals can face criminal charges, as occurred with Bernard von NotHaus, who was convicted in the U.S. for creating Liberty Dollars. [32](https://www.nytimes.com/2012/10/25/us/liberty-dollar-creator-awaits-his-fate-behind-bars.html) [33](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_von_NotHaus) Satoshi may have feared similar prosecution and therefore remained anonymous and withdrew from public view. Gavin Andresen, who worked closely with Satoshi on the Bitcoin software, suggested that Satoshi may have been concerned about legal repercussions from the outset. In the Newsweek article mentioned earlier, Andresen noted that “Back then, it was not clear that creating Bitcoin might be a legal thing to do. He went to great lengths to protect his anonymity.”

### Protecting oneself as a wealthy person <a href="#protecting-oneself-as-a-wealthy-person" id="protecting-oneself-as-a-wealthy-person"></a>

Satoshi Nakamoto amassed approximately one million bitcoins, and these holdings were already increasing in value when Satoshi disappeared in 2012. Today, they would be worth over $100 billion. Depending on where this individual is located, someone with that level of wealth would likely go to great lengths to remain secure and protect against potential threats such as theft, kidnapping, and the risk to personal safety that accompanies extraordinary financial resources.

### Protecting oneself from any retribution after moving markets <a href="#protecting-oneself-from-any-retribution-after-moving-markets" id="protecting-oneself-from-any-retribution-after-moving-markets"></a>

If Satoshi Nakamoto were to sell or “dump” the bitcoins associated with these early holdings, it would almost certainly cause a major drop in bitcoin’s price. Such a dramatic market impact could provoke anger, retaliation, or revenge from those who suffer losses as a result. Remaining anonymous gives Satoshi the flexibility to move or liquidate holdings without facing retribution from affected market participants.

### To redirect attention to the currency and platform <a href="#to-redirect-attention-to-the-currency-and-platform" id="to-redirect-attention-to-the-currency-and-platform"></a>

Some argue that Satoshi Nakamoto may have disappeared to shift attention away from the individual or group behind Bitcoin. Excess focus on a single figure would run counter to Bitcoin’s decentralized philosophy. Any public statements from Satoshi would likely wield outsized influence over the market, overshadowing the voices of the broader Bitcoin community. To avoid this centralization of authority or influence, it is argued that the wisest choice was for the creator to disappear entirely.

### Protection from the middleman who is eliminated <a href="#protection-from-the-middleman-who-is-eliminated" id="protection-from-the-middleman-who-is-eliminated"></a>

Blockchain technology removes the need for intermediaries. These intermediaries—banks, payment services such as Western Union and PayPal, and investment banks—represent a massive industry. Intermediaries stand to lose money as more people adopt bitcoin. For example, Western Union and similar companies were projected to lose $400 million as a result of bitcoin being legalized in El Salvador.[35](https://www.cnbc.com/2021/09/09/el-salvador-bitcoin-move-could-cost-western-union-400-million-a-year.html) Individuals employed by intermediaries also face potential income losses as blockchain technology streamlines and replaces their roles. According to a report from Accenture,[36](https://www.accenture.com/_acnmedia/Accenture/Conversion-Assets/DotCom/Documents/Global/PDF/Consulting/Accenture-Banking-on-Blockchain.pdf) blockchain could enable significant cost savings for investment banks—70 percent in central finance reporting, 50 percent in business operations, 30–50 percent in compliance, and 50 percent in centralized operations. Such efficiencies imply lower profits or lost earnings for many actors in traditional finance.

In short, blockchain technology threatens a large number of stakeholders. If you were an intermediary—or someone whose job depended on processes that blockchain renders obsolete—would you be pleased with Satoshi Nakamoto? It is not hard to imagine intermediaries or displaced individuals attempting to retaliate or even push for criminal charges. People can be deeply vindictive when their livelihoods are threatened. Because blockchain technology disrupts entire industries and traditional roles, Satoshi Nakamoto would have had strong motivation to remain hidden to avoid retaliation by those who stand to lose money or influence as a result of Bitcoin’s creation.
