# Preventing Double Spending on the Bitcoin Platform, Part 2

## **Introduction** <a href="#introduction" id="introduction"></a>

In earlier classes, we explained that when Alice broadcasts her transaction to the network, it contains both the inputs she is using and the outputs she is creating. We identified her inputs as UTXOs from previous transactions, unlocked through her digital signature and public key. Until now, we left an important issue unaddressed: how do we ensure that nothing in the transaction is altered between the time Alice prepares it and the time it is received by the community? That question must be resolved.

We also discussed the mechanisms used on the Bitcoin network to prevent double spending, including proof of work and the rules for appending blocks to the blockchain. Toward the end of that discussion, we noted the possibility that Alice might try to alter a previous block containing one of her own transactions by changing an output so that funds originally sent to someone else are redirected to herself. This would allow her to double spend. We explained that such an attack is difficult because she would need not only to alter the information in the old block but also to mine an entirely new and longer blockchain to replace the existing one. However, we left ambiguous _why_ she must mine new blocks in addition to altering the old one. Understanding this point is crucial because it reveals why changing information on the blockchain is so difficult: each new block includes a verification that the previous block has not been modified. Thus, immutability arises from the requirement that every new block contains evidence that all previous blocks remain intact.

Therefore, two layers of verification must occur: verification that the transaction Alice sent is legitimate, and verification that no prior block in the blockchain has been altered. At this point, we already understand something essential about verification: ensuring that data has not been modified requires hashing the content and providing the hash to a verifier in a secure way. The verifier then hashes the content independently and checks whether both hashes match.

On the Bitcoin network, hashing operates at both the transaction level and the block level. We will examine each of these levels in turn. As we do so, the nature of the mathematical puzzle used in proof of work will also become clear.

## **Transaction level hashing** <a href="#transaction-level-hashing" id="transaction-level-hashing"></a>

Transaction-level hashing serves two purposes. First, it increases the difficulty of altering information stored on the blockchain. Second, it makes detecting unauthorized changes to a transaction much easier. Fully understanding how this hashing makes unauthorized modifications difficult will take time, but we can immediately understand how it enables rapid detection of tampering.

Consider Alice’s transaction in which she pays 0.015 BTC to Bob. You can view it [here](https://bitaps.com/0627052b6f28912f2703066a912ea577f2ce4da4caa5a5fbd8a57286c345c2f2). Alice’s address is 1Cdid9KFAaatwczBwBttQcwXYCpvK8h7FK and Bob’s address is 1GdK9UzpHBzqzX2A9JFP3Di4weBwqgmoQA.

Recall the earlier discussion about Alice’s digital signature. When she signs the transaction, she proves to Bob (and the rest of the network) that she holds the private key corresponding to the address from which the 0.015 BTC is being spent. A valid digital signature requires a message, which is hashed and then encrypted with her private key. We previously stated that this message is assembled from certain components of the transaction she is constructing. Now is the appropriate time to examine a transaction in detail and understand precisely which parts of it are used to generate the message needed for the digital signature.

At a high level, a transaction consists of three components: inputs, outputs, and fees. Although a transaction may contain multiple inputs, it is easiest to begin by focusing on one input. An input contains several pieces of information, which can be viewed by revisiting [Alice’s transaction](https://bitaps.com/0627052b6f28912f2703066a912ea577f2ce4da4caa5a5fbd8a57286c345c2f2). The first component indicates the source of the bitcoins being spent, though the address and amount shown on the page can be misleading, as we will later see how the actual source is referenced. The second component is the SigScript. Clicking the “Display scripts” button reveals two new sections: one for input scripts and one for output scripts. The SigScript, found in the input section, contains Alice’s digital signature (the long string beginning with “30…”), her public key (the long string beginning with “0484…”), and the small pieces of code (such as OP\_PUSHBYTES\[73] and OP\_PUSHBYTES\[65]) that provide the logic required to unlock the bitcoins she is attempting to spend.

A transaction may include multiple outputs as well, and Alice’s transaction has two. Each output specifies the destination address and the amount of bitcoin being sent to that address. It also contains programming conditions that the receiver must satisfy to unlock the funds. We have so far assumed these conditions simply require a digital signature and the corresponding public key, although other conditions are possible, which we will not explore here.

Finally, transactions include fees. A fee is not listed explicitly anywhere in a transaction; instead, it is implied. The fee is simply the difference between the total amount of the inputs and the total amount of the outputs.

### Examining a raw transaction <a href="#examining-a-raw-transaction" id="examining-a-raw-transaction"></a>

The transaction we previously examined was a simplified representation and did not reveal the full structure of a Bitcoin transaction. To understand it in detail, we now turn to its raw form. We will refer to the raw version of **Alice's transaction with Bob** (available [here](https://bitaps.com/0627052b6f28912f2703066a912ea577f2ce4da4caa5a5fbd8a57286c345c2f2)). By selecting the “Raw transaction” link in the bottom right-hand corner of the Transaction box and copying the displayed contents into a document, we obtain a long hexadecimal string that represents the complete transaction. The light red portion corresponds to the input section of the transaction, while the light green portion corresponds to the output section.

We begin by locating the **addresses to which the outputs are sent**. As shown [here](https://bitaps.com/0627052b6f28912f2703066a912ea577f2ce4da4caa5a5fbd8a57286c345c2f2), the outputs are sent to two addresses: 1GdK9UzpHBzqzX2A9JFP3Di4weBwqgmoQA and 1Cdid9KFAaatwczBwBttQcwXYCpvK8h7FK. These familiar base58 addresses do not appear in the raw transaction because the raw format uses hexadecimal representations instead. To convert the base58 addresses into their hexadecimal form, we use the tool at [https://www.better-converter.com/Encoders-Decoders/Base58Check-to-Hexadecimal-Decoder](https://www.better-converter.com/Encoders-Decoders/Base58Check-to-Hexadecimal-Decoder). Decoding Bob’s address produces 00ab68025513c3dbd2f7b92a94e0581f5d50f654e7211d5b87, and decoding Alice’s address produces 007f9b1a7fb68d60c536c2fd8aeaa53a8f3cc025a801974ac6. Removing the first two and last eight characters from each yields the essential address components: ab68025513c3dbd2f7b92a94e0581f5d50f654e7 and 7f9b1a7fb68d60c536c2fd8aeaa53a8f3cc025a8. These fragments can then be located within the raw transaction.

Next, we look for the **address from which the input is sourced**. The input is ultimately coming from Alice’s address, which also receives the change output. The hexadecimal form of this address, after stripping the additional characters as before, is 7f9b1a7fb68d60c536c2fd8aeaa53a8f3cc025a8. Although this value appears in the output portion of the raw transaction, it cannot be found within the input section. This happens because the input of a raw Bitcoin transaction does not directly specify the address spending the bitcoins. Instead, it specifies the transaction output being consumed as input. The address appears only in the earlier transaction in which that output was created.

To see which transaction output is providing funds to Alice, click the < symbol next to the only input in [Alice’s transaction with Bob](https://bitaps.com/0627052b6f28912f2703066a912ea577f2ce4da4caa5a5fbd8a57286c345c2f2/input/0). This leads to the prior transaction from which Alice received the bitcoins. That transaction, found at [this link](https://bitaps.com/7957a35fe64f80d234d76d83a2a8f1a0d8149a41d81de548f0a65a8a999f6f18/output/0), has only one output, labeled output 0, which Alice is now spending. Its transaction ID is 7957a35fe64f80d234d76d83a2a8f1a0d8149a41d81de548f0a65a8a999f6f18. Searching for this exact ID in the raw transaction fails because Bitcoin stores such identifiers in **little-endian** format, while humans normally read them in **big-endian** order. To convert it, we use the tool at [https://blockchain-academy.hs-mittweida.de/litte-big-endian-converter/](https://blockchain-academy.hs-mittweida.de/litte-big-endian-converter/) and specify that the input is hexadecimal. Swapping the endianness produces 186f9f998a5aa6f048e51dd8419a14d8a0f1a8a2836dd734d2804fe65fa35779, which now appears clearly within the raw transaction. Immediately following it, a value indicates which specific output of the earlier transaction is being consumed—here, it is output 0.

Other highlighted regions in the raw transaction indicate the **amounts in the outputs** of Alice's transaction, shown in blue and pink. These simply demonstrate the raw encoding of values that we earlier viewed in a user-friendly format.

We then turn to the **digital signature** included by Alice. When examining [Alice’s transaction](https://bitaps.com/0627052b6f28912f2703066a912ea577f2ce4da4caa5a5fbd8a57286c345c2f2), we saw the following signature:

3045022100884d142d86652a3f47ba4746ec719bbfbd040a570b1deccbb6498c75c4ae24cb02204b9f039ff08df09cbe9f6addac960298cad530a863ea8f53982c09db8f6e381301

This signature appears in the raw transaction exactly as expected, along with Alice’s public key. Understanding how this signature is constructed requires identifying the message that is hashed before being encrypted with Alice’s private key. This message is derived from several parts of the transaction: information about the UTXO being spent (including the originating transaction ID, the output number, the destination address, and the amount), Alice’s public key, and the complete output section of her new transaction. The digital signature itself cannot be part of the message, since the message must be created before the signature can be produced.

After preparing the digital signature, it is combined with the remaining elements of the input and with all outputs to form the complete raw transaction. Bitcoin then applies SHA-256 **twice** to this raw transaction. The hexadecimal raw transaction (after converting it to binary internally) is hashed once, producing an intermediate hash, and hashed again using SHA-256 to produce the final value. By pasting the raw transaction into [https://emn178.github.io/online-tools/double\_sha256.html](https://emn178.github.io/online-tools/double_sha256.html) and choosing HEX as the input type, we obtain the result:

f2c245c38672a5d8fba5a5caa44dcef277a52e916a0603272f91286f2b052706

This output is given in big-endian form. Converting it to little-endian using the converter at [https://blockchain-academy.hs-mittweida.de/litte-big-endian-converter/](https://blockchain-academy.hs-mittweida.de/litte-big-endian-converter/) yields:

0627052b6f28912f2703066a912ea577f2ce4da4caa5a5fbd8a57286c345c2f2

This value, in little-endian format, is exactly the **transaction ID (TxID)** of Alice’s payment to Bob. Thus, a transaction’s ID is simply the double-SHA256 hash of its raw form.

## **Block level hashing** <a href="#block-level-hashing" id="block-level-hashing"></a>

### **What does a block consist of?** <a href="#what-does-a-block-consist-of" id="what-does-a-block-consist-of"></a>

To understand the structure of a block, let us begin by examining an actual example. You can view the **200000th block** in the Bitcoin blockchain [here](https://www.blockchain.com/btc/block/00000000000003a20def7a05a77361b9657ff954b2f2080e135ea6f5970da215). We know this is the 200000th block by looking at its height, which is listed as 199999. The height of a block refers to the number of blocks that precede it. Thus, the first block has height 0, the second block has height 1, and so forth, with each block’s height increasing by one as the chain grows.

### **What do you see in a block?** <a href="#what-do-you-see-in-a-block" id="what-do-you-see-in-a-block"></a>

A block contains a variety of information. One of the main components is the set of transactions included within it. The first transaction is always the COINBASE transaction. When a mining node assembles a block, it begins by inserting this coinbase transaction, which designates the reward the miner will earn if they successfully solve the mathematical puzzle. The block also implicitly includes the fees from all transactions in that block. The presence of the coinbase transaction does not indicate that the miner has already solved the puzzle; rather, it states that if the miner does solve the puzzle, the block it broadcasts will include a transaction awarding the block reward and fees to the miner’s own address.

Each transaction in the block is identified by a transaction ID, and we already know that this ID is simply the hash of the transaction’s contents.

| <p>Note </p><p>A coinbase output is considered spendable only after 100 confirmations. This rule has been part of the Bitcoin protocol since its first version, released by Satoshi Nakamoto in 2009. It is a consensus rule, not merely a convention, and is enforced by every full node. The purpose of the 100-block maturity period is to protect against chain reorganizations that could invalidate a miner’s reward if it were spent too soon. Ordinary transactions are usually considered final after about six confirmations because, at a practical level, the chance of their exclusion from the longest chain becomes negligible. However, for coinbase transactions, the protocol demands even greater certainty that the block containing the coinbase transaction will remain part of the longest chain. If a miner attempts to spend a coinbase output before it matures, the transaction will be rejected by the network.</p> |

Beyond the transaction list, a block also displays several additional pieces of information, such as the total number of transactions it contains and the miner’s reward, which includes both the block reward for solving the puzzle and the accumulated fees from all included transactions.

Much of this information can also be viewed in a JSON format, which is particularly useful because that is the structure in which we will later examine blocks. You can see the 200000th block in JSON format by clicking [here](https://blockchain.info/block-height/199999?format=json). The raw JSON data is initially difficult to interpret, so it helps to format it. If your browser provides a “Pretty-print” option, enable it and review the structured output. If it does not, you can copy the JSON link into an online viewer. For example:

It is important to recognize that the information shown on these websites includes summary fields generated by the viewing platform. These summaries are derived from the real block data but are not part of the block itself. To understand block-level hashing, we must now narrow our focus to what is _actually_ stored inside a block.

### **How is a block created?** <a href="#how-is-a-block-created" id="how-is-a-block-created"></a>

A block is composed of the transactions that a mining node has verified, along with a block header that both summarizes these transactions and links the block to its predecessor. If you were acting as a miner, the process of creating a block would unfold in several steps. You would begin by assembling the transactions that will appear in the block. This collection always starts with the coinbase transaction, and it may include other transactions your node has received for verification. As a miner, you may choose whether to include transactions that do not carry fees, and you may even choose to include nothing beyond the coinbase transaction—though doing so would cause you to forfeit any fees that other transactions might have provided. You are free to include invalid or unverifiable transactions, but doing so would be self-defeating. If your block is mined successfully but contains transactions that other nodes cannot verify, the block will be rejected when you broadcast it. Because of this, it is in your best interest to include only valid and verifiable transactions.

Once the transactions are assembled, you create the block header. This must be done afterward because the header incorporates information derived from the transactions themselves. After preparing the header, you join it with the collection of transactions to form the complete block, which is now ready for mining. To better understand this process, we construct a block header using the 200000th block, available in JSON format [here](https://blockchain.info/block-height/199999?format=json).

A visual overview of what appears in a block header is provided in the figure sourced from [here](https://www.datadriveninvestor.com/2019/11/21/a-decomposition-of-the-bitcoin-block-header/). The diagram serves only to familiarize you with the structure; we will soon explore each element in detail. The header begins with the Version field, which specifies the block validation rules to be followed. This version number has evolved over time alongside updates to the Bitcoin software. The header also includes the Previous block hash, which is the hash of the preceding block’s header. Another important field is the Merkle root, a condensed summary of all transactions in the block. The Timestamp records the time at which the miner began attempting to solve the puzzle. The Difficulty target, also called “bits,” represents the threshold that the hash of the block header must be less than or equal to. Finally, the Nonce is the value that miners adjust repeatedly in their attempt to solve the puzzle.

### **What is the Merkle Root?** <a href="#what-is-the-merkle-root" id="what-is-the-merkle-root"></a>

The Merkle root is best understood by actually computing one. To do this, we follow a procedure similar in logic to the one used in the Bitcoin protocol, though we intentionally avoid certain formatting transformations that the protocol applies. These transformations, while important in real mining, would only distract us from the underlying concept here. Skipping them means the numerical Merkle root we obtain will differ from the real one, but the idea remains identical. We also simplify the process further by using only four transactions rather than all the transactions in a block.

To begin, you create Sheet 2 in your spreadsheet. Then you visit the Google Sheets spreadsheet available [here](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1JAnuiyGUawCh6vY1gPThhdb-ybwxXr2qoYor3-ENIao/edit?usp=sharing) and copy its contents into Sheet 2. After adjusting the formatting, you move to the block information for the 200000th block at [https://blockstream.info/block/00000000000003a20def7a05a77361b9657ff954b2f2080e135ea6f5970da215](https://blockstream.info/block/00000000000003a20def7a05a77361b9657ff954b2f2080e135ea6f5970da215) and locate the list of transactions. You copy the first four TXIDs into the appropriate cells of your sheet. These TXIDs are simply the hashes of the transactions, something we had discussed earlier.

You then concatenate pairs of TXIDs, double-hash each concatenated pair, and repeat the process up the tree. Eventually, this produces a single value at the top, which is the Merkle root of the four transactions. Your results should match the example shown [here](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1hlfnMYR4N3xiwnH33iOBhJW4iJ0WvJCQ/view?usp=sharing), where the root appears as df322475c740a3eca447a20402cb98b6b3da5bb4e47d9127aca64704140f5465. This exercise captures the core idea: TXIDs are paired, concatenated, and double-hashed repeatedly until only one value remains. If there is an odd number of items at any step, the final one is duplicated to allow pairing. This process continues layer by layer until the Merkle root is produced.

### **What have we achieved with the Merkle root?** <a href="#what-have-we-achieved-with-the-merkle-root" id="what-have-we-achieved-with-the-merkle-root"></a>

When you look at the spreadsheet in which you computed the Merkle root, you can see that it is a sort of a unique summary of the four transactions. If anything in any of the four transactions were to change, the hash of that transaction would change. If the hash of any of the four transactions changes, the Merkle root would change too. Thus, the Merkle root is a compact summarization of all the transactions in a block. If that changes, it means that one or more transactions have changed.

### **What have we achieved by incorporating the hash of the previous block?** <a href="#what-have-we-achieved-by-incorporating-the-hash-of-the-previous-block" id="what-have-we-achieved-by-incorporating-the-hash-of-the-previous-block"></a>

By including the hash of the previous block in the current block’s header, the protocol makes it extremely difficult to alter any information from earlier blocks. Because the previousblockhash reflects the combined hash of fields such as version, previousblockhash, merkleroot, time, bits, and nonce, any change to a transaction in that earlier block would alter its Merkle root, which would alter its block hash. That would invalidate the previousblockhash stored in the next block, forcing someone to recompute that block as well. The effect cascades: changing a block several layers deep requires recomputing every block that follows it. Consequently, the deeper a block sits in the chain, the harder it becomes to tamper with its contents. This effect is illustrated in the figure available [here](https://drive.google.com/file/d/13D6qhLlADNLZ3s9TlEqzRWSCrwXyB8UP/view?usp=sharing).

#### What is the target? <a href="#what-is-the-target" id="what-is-the-target"></a>

The target is used to **derive a number** that the block hash cannot equal or exceed for the mining process to be considered as successful. In other words, when a miner is mining a block and is creating new block hashes by changing the nonce, we say that the miner has been successful at mining the block when the block hash is less than the number derived from the target.

We will call the number derived from the target as the target value.

In sum, the target determines the target value through a complex formula. The target value is important because the block has to be less than the target value for one to say that they have solved the mathematical puzzle.

The Bitcoin protocol has set the maximum target value to that which can be derived from a target equal to 486604799 in decimal (i.e., 1d00ffff in hex) ([source](https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Difficulty)). There is no minimum target but because of how the target value is computed, we know that the target value will always be positive.

#### Recomputing the target <a href="#recomputing-the-target" id="recomputing-the-target"></a>

The target value is recomputed every 2016 blocks using the following formula (see [related article](https://learnmeabitcoin.com/technical/target)):

new target value

\= old target value x (average time in minutes required to mine the last 2016 blocks ÷ 10)

If the average time to mine the last 2016 blocks is more than 10 minutes, then the new target value will be higher than the old value.

This means that the range of acceptable block hashes is larger. In other words, it becomes easier to mine. Which means that it would take less time to mine a block. That is the desired effect.

If the average time to mine the last 2016 blocks is less than 10 minutes, then the new target value will be lower than the old value.

This means that the range of acceptable block hashes is smaller. In other words, it becomes harder to mine. Which means that it would take more time to mine a block. That is the desired effect.

### **51% attack** <a href="#id-51-attack" id="id-51-attack"></a>

The structure of a block makes tampering with the blockchain extraordinarily difficult. If someone alters any transaction in block 1 after it has already been accepted, the change disrupts the prevblockhash field in block 2, because that field encodes the exact hash of block 1. Once block 2 must be modified, its previous nonce—the value that originally satisfied the mining puzzle—no longer works, forcing the entire mining process for block 2 to be redone. The same chain reaction propagates forward: changing block 2 breaks block 3, changing block 3 breaks block 4, and so on. To successfully tamper with any block, an attacker must remine that block _and every block after it_ to rebuild a chain long enough to replace the legitimate one.

This challenge is compounded by how the Bitcoin protocol selects the authoritative blockchain. Since many mining nodes produce blocks simultaneously, nodes always adopt the longest valid chain they encounter. A miner working on block x+1 must abandon their work if another miner publishes a valid chain that already includes x+1 blocks. This means that anyone attempting to falsify the ledger must not only rebuild all blocks from the point of alteration but must also extend the chain by at least one additional block so that the tampered chain is longer than the honest one. Only then will the network accept it.

While this seems nearly impossible, it is not if the attacker controls more computational power than the rest of the network combined. A miner with more than 50% of total hashing power can, with high probability, outrun the honest network and privately extend a fraudulent chain until it becomes longer. This scenario is known as a **51% attack**.

The figures from [https://blog.goodaudience.com/what-is-a-51-attack-or-double-spend-attack-aa108db63474](https://blog.goodaudience.com/what-is-a-51-attack-or-double-spend-attack-aa108db63474) illustrate the danger. Imagine a user who spends 100 bitcoins on an expensive yacht but privately mines an alternate chain in which the same 100 bitcoins are instead sent back to an address they control. In the public blockchain, the purchase appears valid. In the private blockchain, the user never spent anything.

<figure><img src=".gitbook/assets/image (32).png" alt=""><figcaption></figcaption></figure>

As the attacker continues mining privately, their superior hashing power allows the parallel chain to grow faster than the public one.

Once the fraudulent chain becomes longer than the honest chain, the attacker broadcasts it. Because the network is programmed to accept the longest valid chain, the fraudulent version replaces the legitimate one. The original payment disappears from history, the buyer keeps both the yacht and the bitcoin, and the network is successfully defrauded.

The exercise also reveals that anyone with 51% or more hashing power would almost certainly succeed in double spending. This scenario is particularly concerning when mining pools become disproportionately large. In 2014, the pool Ghash.io briefly exceeded 50% of the Bitcoin network’s hash power. A 51% attack was technically possible, yet it did not occur.

Ghash.io publicly stated they would not attack the network, since doing so would destroy Bitcoin’s value and their own long-term profits. Their statement, available [here](https://www.ccn.com/bitcoin-mining-pool-ghash-io-is-unapologetic-risk-theoretical-51-attack/), emphasized that harming Bitcoin would be against their economic interests. On multiple occasions when Ghash.io neared 50%, miners voluntarily left the pool to reduce systemic risk. One such mining firm, BitFury, withdrew power and made the public statement quoted in [this article](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2014/jun/16/bitcoin-currency-destroyed-51-attack-ghash-io).

Launching a real 51% attack is extremely expensive. Although sites like [https://www.crypto51.app/](https://www.crypto51.app/) suggest the opposite, their estimates assume large amounts of rentable hash power. In reality, Bitcoin-compatible hardware cannot simply be rented at scale. Buying the necessary specialized hardware requires astronomical capital: around 1.2 million ASIC miners at roughly $10,000 each, totaling around $12 billion, plus electricity costs, staffing, and infrastructure. Estimates such as those at [https://gobitcoin.io/tools/cost-51-attack/](https://gobitcoin.io/tools/cost-51-attack/) point to this scale.

Even if a nation-state attempted such an attack, the community has countermeasures. A large deployment of new mining hardware cannot be hidden; network participants monitor hashrate distribution continuously and would detect unusual increases. Exchanges could freeze activity, mining pools could reject blocks from suspicious sources, and the community could coordinate stronger measures. The “point of exit” problem also prevents attackers from easily converting double-spent funds into fiat money through regulated exchanges enforcing KYC/AML. Monitoring organizations such as MIT’s Digital Currency Initiative, described in this [article](https://cointelegraph.com/news/what-is-a-51-attack-and-how-to-detect-it), actively track anomalies such as deep reorganizations or unusually long competing chains.

In extreme circumstances, pools may “shun” an attacker by refusing to build on their blocks, ISPs may shut down malicious infrastructure, and the community could even coordinate an emergency hard fork to invalidate the attacker’s ASICs—an undesirable but powerful deterrent. Although theoretically a country with large mining operations (historically China, now more commonly the US) could attempt a 51% attack, miners can instantly redirect their hash power to safer pools, as described in [this piece](https://cointelegraph.com/news/why-chinese-miners-wont-stage-a-51-attack-on-bitcoin).

While such attacks are extremely difficult on Bitcoin due to its size, they are far more feasible on smaller blockchains, which can be compromised with much lower cost or via rented hash power. A discussion of this phenomenon is available in [this article](https://www.coindesk.com/blockchains-feared-51-attack-now-becoming-regular).

### **Why do we say that the transactions in xth last block have x confirmations?** <a href="#why-do-we-say-that-the-transactions-in-xth-last-block-have-x-confirmations" id="why-do-we-say-that-the-transactions-in-xth-last-block-have-x-confirmations"></a>

To understand confirmations, consider the figure shown below ([larger figure](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/16nZwwrWhyLlEZvCudnYtkkXwsloWXVjrakNNhHOKQNc/edit?usp=sharing)).

<figure><img src=".gitbook/assets/image (36).png" alt=""><figcaption></figcaption></figure>

When a new block is added to the blockchain, the transactions inside it receive their first confirmation. This confirmation comes from the block’s own hash, which acts as a cryptographic seal on its contents. When the next block is appended and includes the previous block’s hash inside its header, the earlier transactions become further solidified. They now have two confirmations: one from their own block, and one from the block that references them.

Every time another block is added, it reinforces all earlier blocks. A transaction in the third-last block therefore has three confirmations; one in the sixth-last block has six confirmations, and so on. Because a block hash depends on everything that came before it, altering a transaction buried six blocks deep would require re-mining that block and every block after it, while simultaneously outpacing the honest network. This is why six confirmations are widely considered the practical standard for irreversibility in Bitcoin: the transaction has been structurally buried under six additional layers of proof-of-work, making tampering extraordinarily unlikely.

Each new block strengthens the entire chain beneath it. The deeper a transaction sits, the harder it becomes to dislodge, providing Bitcoin with its layered model of security and finality.
